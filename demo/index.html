Forecasting GPU rental prices by looking at the offers behind each price

Question. What will posted GPU rental prices be 1, 3 and 7 days after 6 November 2026, and how sure can we be?

Result. On 555 other price series, checked on the held-back weeks for the first time, our forecast scored 0.228 against 0.102 for the organisers' baseline (difference +0.126, rough range +0.115 to +0.134). On the 37 series that will be scored on Sunday it scored 0.214 against 0.084, but that number flatters us: 4 of the 7 models in the mix were kept because they did well on those same weeks. Both were measured on 18 held-back days, 13 to 30 October. The score says how much better we are than assuming prices stay where they are; 0 is no better. The ranges are rough: the held-back period is only 3 weeks, so resampling whole weeks gives just 10 distinct draws.

The idea. A posted GPU price is the middle of a short list of offers from different providers (3 to 17 of them here). It changes only when a provider near the middle changes its price, or when an offer joins or leaves the list. The system found this by studying where its own forecasts went wrong. It then stopped forecasting the price as a line and started simulating the list: each offer moves the way its provider's prices moved over the last 21 days, and the forecast is the middle of the simulated list.

Words used on this page
Offer
One provider's posted price for one GPU setup on one day, in US dollars per GPU-hour.
The list and its middle
The short list of provider offers behind each published price, and its middle value (the median). The published price is exactly that middle value.
Score
How much better than assuming prices stay where they are: 1 minus our error divided by the error of repeating the latest price. 0 is no better. The error counts the low, middle and high estimate.
Low, middle and high estimate
We give three numbers per forecast: 1 in 10 chance the price ends below the low one, 1 in 10 above the high one, and the middle one in between.
Organisers' baseline
The latest price, widened by how much that series has moved in the past (called "drift" in the starter kit).
Practice dates and held-back weeks
We built and compared ideas on the practice dates (18 August to 6 October). The held-back weeks (13 to 30 October) were looked at once per idea, after writing down what we would test.
Forecast date and days ahead
The forecast date is the last day of data a forecast may use (6 November for Sunday). Days ahead is 1, 3 or 7.
Scored series
The 37 series the organisers will score, picked by their rule as of 6 November (the "headline tier").
Our first model
Our first idea that beat the baseline: borrow price history from similar series and widen the range when the price moved recently.
Provider simulator
Moves each offer the way its provider moved prices over the last 21 days and takes the middle of the simulated list, so a climbing provider pulls the price up only until it passes the next offer.

What to keep in mind

  • Sunday's scoring is one forecast date. On 50 practice dates the mix beat the organisers' baseline on 46 and did worse than assuming no change on 2. Neighbouring dates share most of their data, so this is weaker than 50 separate tests; one bad week is possible.
  • The scored series were picked with hindsight. They are the ones that qualify on 6 November. Applying the organisers' rule date by date instead, our first model's lead over the baseline is +0.033 on the forecasts that qualified at the time, against +0.080 on the fixed set. Numbers on the scored series are flattered by this.
  • Only 2 of the 4 new models clearly hold on the held-back weeks. The test we planned is too easy to pass with only 18 days. Under the audit's stricter version: the provider simulator and the Chronos range from the last 16 days, held under both versions; the jump estimates (where the middle goes if one offer leaves or joins), held under one of two; similar past situations, held under neither. The unproven ones stay in the mix because leaving either out lowered the score on the practice dates; that is a departure from our original rule.
  • The audit found one small leak, now fixed. One model (similar past situations) could be nudged by data from after the forecast date: at most 0.0021% of a price (2.1e-5 in log) on forecasts that were used, and not at all on Sunday's date, which has no later data. Every other model (Chronos range, organisers' baseline, 16-day Chronos range, jump estimates, boosted trees, first model, provider simulator, provider simulator with calendar) gave identical forecasts when all later data was deleted or scrambled; two deliberately cheating test models were caught in 36 of 36 checks.
  • The announcement calendar's gain rests on 2 events (Jarvislabs on 5 Oct 2026, Nebius on 1 Oct 2026) and is switched off in the submitted forecast.
  • The 2 to 3 year view gives ranges, not a tested forecast. No version of the method beat assuming no change on any backtest.
  • Who did the work. Ideas and forecasting code were written by LLM research agents, directed by an orchestrator agent and a human. Each step of the research loop is a command (python -m gpuf.loop), but choosing and writing a new idea is not automated.

Every idea we tested, and what happened

37 ideas: 13 worked, 15 did not, 7 made no clear difference, 2 could not be tested. Each was written down with what would count as failure before it was tested, and the failures stay in the list. Rows where the loop tool re-ran an earlier idea are left out. Open "Exact record" for the original wording.

Showing all 37 ideas.

  1. did not work H1

    Prices wobble around a slow-moving level, so averaging the last few days should beat using the latest price.

    Averaging made the guess worse: errors were 10% to 60% bigger than just using the latest price.

    Exact record

    Claim: Daily medians are a slow level plus noise, so a smoothed level beats the last value as the centre.

    Result: Loss-weighted error ratio against the last value 1.6 (h=1), 1.2 (h=3), 1.1 (h=7) on the 37 scored series; no mean reversion on afhubbard.

    Tested by the eda agent. Evidence: research-log.md 10:50; experiments/00-eda

  2. worked H2

    A price that changed recently is more likely to change again.

    Using this beat the organisers' baseline by 0.08 on the practice dates. On its own it did not clearly hold on the held-back weeks (+0.03); it did as part of a mix.

    Exact record

    Claim: A series that changed recently is more likely to move again; conditioning the change distribution on that state improves intervals.

    Result: mix with state chgk3: +0.072 over drift [+0.054,+0.086]; with recency +0.081 [+0.057,+0.093] (dev headline, 4,278 cells). Validation alone +0.028 [-0.006,+0.051], not significant; in a blend with gbm +0.069 [+0.051,+0.083].

    Tested by the mix agent. Evidence: research-log.md 11:20, 12:10; experiments/mix

  3. worked H3

    Most series are only two or three months old, so borrow history from similar series.

    +0.045 over the baseline, but only because it fills in for series too new for the baseline to handle.

    Exact record

    Claim: Per-series change quantiles are noisy on short histories; pooling by collector and class fixes it.

    Result: +0.045 over drift [+0.036,+0.059] overall, 0.150 against 0.153 on cells where drift is live: pooling fixes the fallback and nothing more (dev headline, 4,278 cells).

    Tested by the mix agent. Evidence: research-log.md 11:20

  4. did not work H4

    When a price jumps because a provider joined or left, it drifts back afterwards.

    It mostly stays where it jumped to. Providers that leave almost never return within a week (0% to 6%).

    Exact record

    Claim: Jumps in a median come from provider-mix changes and partly revert.

    Result: Composition jumps mostly do not revert: slope of the next 7-day change on the jump -0.16 (90% CI -0.30 to -0.14, n=56); exited offers return within 7 days in 5.6% of 162 fastgpu exits and 0 of 118 gpurentalprices exits. Repricing: momentum on fastgpu (+0.40 at h=3, CI 0.19 to 0.77, n=124), reversion on gpurentalprices (-0.46 at h=7, CI -0.69 to -0.19, n=256) driven by marketplace asks. Dev offer rows to 2026-10-13.

    Tested by the autopsy agent. Evidence: experiments/autopsy/results/step3_summary.json

  6. worked H6

    Off-the-shelf forecasting models (Chronos, TimesFM) cannot say where these prices go, but may judge how uncertain they are.

    Their guess of the price itself was worse than the baseline. Their sense of how wide the range should be was good: +0.12 over the baseline on the held-back weeks.

    Exact record

    Claim: Pretrained time-series models carry no usable signal on administered step-like prices.

    Result: Supported for the centre, refuted for the width. Raw quantiles: Chronos-Bolt -0.050, TimesFM -0.055 against drift. Last value as centre with the model's width: +0.055 and +0.061 (dev headline, 4,278 cells); on validation Chronos width +0.121 over drift [+0.071,+0.157], the best single method.

    Tested by the foundation agent. Evidence: research-log.md 11:20, 12:10

  11. worked H11

    Each published price is the middle of a short list of provider offers, so every move comes from a provider changing its price, joining or leaving.

    We can rebuild the published price exactly from the offers. 86% of moves are a provider repricing and 10% are a provider joining or leaving, but joining and leaving caused about a third of our forecast error. The size of those jumps can be read off the list.

    Exact record

    Claim: Every median jump is a repriced offer near the middle of the panel or a change in panel membership.

    Result: Median reproduced from offers on 100% of 2,483 series-days. 579 changes since 4 Aug: 86% repricing, 10% composition, 4% mixed (an independent count by the panel agent on 33 series: 84%, 6%, 10%). Share of the blend's dev loss: composition 32%, mixed 22%, Verda's steady increases 17.5%, one marketplace ask series 8.9%, hyperscaler spot 6.8%. A composition jump equals the panel's one-offer fragility (correlation 0.88, n=60); its new value is a neighbouring order statistic or midpoint in 79% of cases at h=1 and 54% at h=7. 60 composition events fall on 19 dates.

    Tested by the autopsy agent. Evidence: experiments/autopsy/results/failure_modes.json

  14. did not work H14

    Our ranges are steadily too narrow on the upside, so widen them automatically.

    The misses came in one stretch of several weeks, not steadily. Automatic widening helped in that stretch and hurt before it.

    Exact record

    Result: The upper-tail gap at h=7 is one multi-week regime, not a stable bias: above-q90 rate 0.110 in the first half of dev and 0.330 in the second (weekly 0.13, 0.02, 0.08, 0.24, 0.38, 0.38, 0.29, 0.08; lag-1 autocorrelation of the daily rate 0.93). Adaptive conformal inference loses (-0.017 to -0.025). Lower-tail correction adds nothing. Not nominated.

Shared memory between agents (past.dev)

The answer in brief

  1. Rent, H100 (median of static neocloud rate cards, 3.49 USD/GPU-h on 6 Nov 2026): 2.3 at +24 months (q10 1.2, q90 4.2), 1.8 at +36 months (0.8 to 3.8).
  2. Rent, A100 80GB (same instrument, 2.35 today): 1.8 at +24 months (1.0 to 3.1), 1.6 at +36 months (0.7 to 3.0).
  3. Resale, income approach: H100 26k USD per GPU now (12k to 54k), 10k at +24 months, 5k at +36 months. A100 9k now (4k to 19k), 2k at +24 months, about 0 at +36 months. The published used figures (H100 18k to 22k, A100 80GB 5k to 9.5k) sit inside the q10 to q90 range and below the H100 median.
  4. Lending: the q10 path supports a loan-to-value of about 65% of the 20k used price on 24 or 36-month H100 loans. That is the low edge of the 60 to 70% advance rates reported in the market. A 35% residual is the median case at 24 months, not a conservative case at 36 months.
  5. Probabilities: shortage persists through 2027, 0.35; eases to the multi-year trend, 0.40; successor glut from 2028, 0.25. No version of the method beat no-change on any backtest. Its intervals cover only when they are made as wide as AWS spot noise.
