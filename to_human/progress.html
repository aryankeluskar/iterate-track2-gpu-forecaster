GPU price forecaster: where we are

Updated Sat 14 Nov, 13:37. 136 tests run, 37 ideas tried.

The short version

  • It works. On three held-back weeks our forecaster scores 0.214 on the 37 scored prices; the organisers' baseline scores 0.084. That number flatters us a little, because some parts of the forecaster were kept for doing well on those same weeks. On the other prices, checked on those weeks for the first time, it scores 0.228 against 0.102.
  • Why it works. Each published GPU price is just the middle of a short list of offers from providers. It moves when a provider near the middle changes its price, or when a provider joins or leaves the list. We forecast that list, not the price line.
  • How we got there. We tried 37 ideas: 13 worked, 15 didn't work, 7 no clear effect, 2 couldn't test. All of them are in the table below, including the ones that failed.
  • What to keep in mind. Sunday's scoring is one single date, so a bad week is possible. An outside check also found that some of our held-back-week claims were too confident. Details are in the table (last row) and in to_human/track2_feasibility.md.
Reading the scores. The score says how much better we are than simply assuming every price stays where it is. Zero means no better, higher is better. "Practice dates" are 18 August to 6 October, which we were free to look at. "Held-back weeks" are 13 to 30 October, which we only looked at to check an idea once it was finished. The "baseline" is the simple method the organisers ask everyone to beat.

Progress on the practice dates

Each grey dot is one test. The blue line is the best score so far and ends at 0.209. The orange circle is one test we do not count: the provider simulation with the calendar of announced price changes switched on. Its jump comes from just two announcements (Nebius on 1 October and Jarvislabs on 5 October), we tuned its timing on those same two, and we could not check it on the held-back weeks. It is switched off in what we submit. Everything on this chart is from the practice dates; results on the held-back weeks are in the table.

Everything we tried

Gains like "+0.03" are in the same score as above. The exact record, with every number and its source, is in data_out/ledger.jsonl and research-log.md.

The ideaDid it work?What happened
1Prices wobble around a slow-moving level, so averaging the last few days should beat using the latest price.Didn't workAveraging made the guess worse: errors were 10% to 60% bigger than just using the latest price.
2A price that changed recently is more likely to change again.WorkedUsing this beat the organisers' baseline by 0.08 on the practice dates. On its own it did not clearly hold on the held-back weeks (+0.03); it did as part of a mix.
3Most series are only two or three months old, so borrow history from similar series.Worked+0.045 over the baseline, but only because it fills in for series too new for the baseline to handle.
4When a price jumps because a provider joined or left, it drifts back afterwards.Didn't workIt mostly stays where it jumped to. Providers that leave almost never return within a week (0% to 6%).
5The most recent day's data gets revised later.Couldn't testWe only have one copy of the data, so there is nothing to compare.
6Off-the-shelf forecasting models (Chronos, TimesFM) cannot say where these prices go, but may judge how uncertain they are.WorkedTheir guess of the price itself was worse than the baseline. Their sense of how wide the range should be was good: +0.12 over the baseline on the held-back weeks.
7The same GPU's price on another data source moves first.Didn't workNudging our guess by the other source's last move never helped.
8If many prices on a data source changed lately, each one will move more next.Didn't workNo version helped.
9After a jump of more than 5%, the price gives some of it back within a week.Didn't workBetting on a pullback made things slightly worse.
10A burst of news headlines about GPU price rises (found with Exa) warns of a jump.No clear effectA tiny gain (+0.01) that came from a single episode. It hurt on more dates than it helped.
11Each published price is the middle of a short list of provider offers, so every move comes from a provider changing its price, joining or leaving.WorkedWe can rebuild the published price exactly from the offers. 86% of moves are a provider repricing and 10% are a provider joining or leaving, but joining and leaving caused about a third of our forecast error. The size of those jumps can be read off the list.
12Simulate the list of offers and take its middle, instead of extrapolating the price.No clear effectThe first version only tied our starting model (0.170 against 0.169). It could not foresee brand-new providers joining.
13Big price changes are announced publicly before they happen.Mostly noOnly 2 of the 37 biggest were announced ahead (Nebius 15 days, Jarvislabs 10 days). Most big moves are marketplace prices that nobody announces.
14Our ranges are steadily too narrow on the upside, so widen them automatically.Didn't workThe misses came in one stretch of several weeks, not steadily. Automatic widening helped in that stretch and hurt before it.
15Learn which method to trust more as results come in.No clear effectLearned weights were no better than a plain average (+0.004). The best method keeps changing, and chasing it loses.
16Predict whether a price will move, then how far.Didn't workWe can tell fairly well whether a price will move, but not which way. The forecaster built on it lost (0.108 against 0.169).
17The Laya decision model can read a price history and say up, flat or down.Didn't workWorse than always guessing the most common outcome, even after recalibration. A simple statistical model on the same numbers did far better. Fine-tuning did not finish because it ran out of memory.
18A pretrained model's sense of uncertainty carries over because it reacts to how jumpy the last couple of weeks were.WorkedGiving it only the last 16 days works best. Mixed with our starting model it scored 0.170 against 0.112 on the held-back weeks, and it passes the stricter check.
19When two data sources disagree on a GPU's price, the gap closes.Didn't workIt does not close in any predictable way. No outside price index helped either.
20Have an AI write a tiny model's weights by hand (weight-space programming) instead of training it.No clear effectThe hand-written weights beat the same model trained the normal way, but did not clearly beat our starting model.
21Find past situations that looked like today, across all 592 series, and see what happened next.Worked, with a caveat+0.03 on the practice dates and +0.02 on the held-back weeks when mixed with the starting model. A stricter check says the held-back result could be luck (about a 1 in 7 chance). The thing that matters most is how many providers are in the list.
22A model built for small tables (TabPFN) beats our tree model.Couldn't testWe stopped it: the laptop was out of memory and this was the least promising heavy job. This says nothing about the method.
23A series that never moves should get no range at all.WorkedA small but steady gain (+0.008) across all series. Fancier rules per price type looked great early and fell apart later.
24Give each provider's offer that provider's own recent pricing habits, then take the middle of the list.WorkedThe first method to predict the price itself better than assuming no change (5% less error, 10% less where a frequent repricer sits in the middle). Mixed with the starting model it scored 0.185 against 0.112 on the held-back weeks, and it passes the stricter check.
25Skip the simulation: just look up who sits in the middle of the list and how often they change price.No clear effectTied the starting model. There are too few past examples to learn from. Simulating works, looking up does not.
26A provider's published pricing policy (found with Exa) tells us how often its price will move.Didn't workAssuming a provider never changes price was a better first guess. Verda says it is ending dynamic pricing but still changes prices on 4 days in 10.
27Build a calendar of announced price changes with Exa and apply each one on its date.Worked on practice dates only17 of 17 announced prices showed up exactly as announced. A big gain on the practice dates (0.283 against 0.209), but from just two announcements, and it could not be checked on the held-back weeks. It is switched off in our submission.
28Put the edges of our range where the price would land if one provider left or joined.Worked, with a caveat0.219 against 0.112 on the held-back weeks, our biggest single gain, though a stricter check calls it borderline. Providers change in batches: right after one change, another is over 20 times more likely.
29Rental prices fall with a chip's age at a steady rate that we can extend two to three years.Didn't workAssuming no change beat it over the past year, because prices rose in 2026 (a shortage year).
30A provider that lists a GPU on one data source will soon show up on the other.No clear effectIt is 17 times more likely than chance, but that is still about a 0.3% chance per day. Not enough to forecast with.
31Our new methods fixed what they were aimed at, and what is left is mostly new providers joining.WorkedError from frequent repricers fell the most. The biggest remaining causes: a new offer joining (29%), frequent repricers (16%), announced one-off changes (14%).
32The provider simulation still holds up if a price trend suddenly stops or reverses.WorkedIn made-up scenarios it kept most of its lead (+0.033 to +0.035, against +0.040 in the real data). We have not seen a real reversal yet.
33A provider's price change shows up on one data source a day before the other.Didn't workChecked before building anything: it happens in only 1 case in 3, and price gaps between sources do not close.
34An equal mix of all seven working methods beats the baseline on the held-back weeks.Worked0.214 against 0.084 on the 37 scored series, and 0.228 against 0.102 on every other series, checked on those weeks for the first time. 80% of prices landed inside our range, and the goal is 80%.
35The provider simulation also works for the big clouds (AWS, Google, Azure, Oracle).Didn't workWith about 450 offers per list, the individual moves cancel out. Their list prices almost never change, so giving them no range is right, and our submission already does that.
36We can give useful two to three year ranges for H100 and A100 rent and resale.Ranges yes, proven accuracy noRanges, yes: H100 rent is $3.49 now and about $2.31 in two years ($1.21 to $4.17). Proven accuracy, no: nothing beat assuming no change when tested on the past.
37Our testing is airtight.Mostly, not fullyAn independent check found one tiny leak, now fixed (it moved forecasts by 0.002% at most). It confirmed our scoring matches the organisers'. It also showed that some of our claims about the held-back weeks were too confident.
